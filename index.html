DEV LOG

How One More Take was made

One More Take is my solo entry for the 2026 Epic AI MegaJam, on the theme Director's Cut. You play a stunt double reshooting one scene, and after every take the director adds something worse. I made it in Unreal Engine 5.9 by prompting AI agents. Here is how it went, from the first pitch to the version you can play, with the prompts I typed along the way.

Play it on itch.io Watch the gameplay video

How I worked

Claude Code did the building. It wrote the gameplay in C++, ran the Unreal editor through its built-in MCP server, and called the tools that made the characters, the art and the sound. I chose what to make, played each version and told it what to change.

The prompts are mine, copied as I typed them, typos and all, and […] marks a cut. Claude wrote the notes around them from the record it kept as it worked.

Part 1

The idea

1Four pitches and a stunt double

The jam's theme was Director's Cut. Claude came back with four pitches for it, and option A was a stunt double who reshoots one scene while the director keeps changing it.

That became the brief: five takes of the same scene. After each take the director adds one note, drawn at random, so no two runs end in the same cut.

Part 2

Finding the look

2Round 1: four films, none of them right

Next came mockups, so I could see the game before any of it existed. I also wanted the moment the director yells "Cut!" to be a show of its own.

Claude drew the same stunt double and the same moment in four films: a rooftop chase, a runaway train, a space station and a jungle ruin. Each row shows the film as you'd play it, the same on a phone, and the studio behind it after "Cut!".

3Round 2: a city, and a better camera

None of the four felt like it could become iconic, and I doubted that a run straight into the screen would catch anyone's eye.

Round 2 tried four city films, each with its own hero and camera: a monster movie, a 1940s detective story in black and white, a 1980s street-market comedy, and a seaside heist in flat poster colours.

4Round 3: black and white until the cut

Two of them pulled me in different directions: the market's colours and the detective's black and white.

Claude tried four cameras on the market street and two versions of the look. One it added without being asked: the detective story shot in the market at night, all grey except the hero and the hazards. When the director yells "Cut!", the studio lights come on and the market's colour floods back. I picked that look, O, and camera J, which chases the hero from behind and to one side.

Part 3

Building it

5The test level

Claude split the work three ways. Other agents started on the hero and the set, while Claude built a test level itself out of placeholder shapes, with all the gameplay in C++, so the rules could be played before any art existed. Less than two hours into the jam, the run, the steering, the jumps and slides, the director's notes and the cut to the set all worked in grey boxes.

To test it without me, Claude gave it a computer player and a script that plays through the game's own controls. 17 of its 18 checks passed. The one that failed showed that the rain made some runs too hard.

6The hero and the cast

The hero started as a character sheet. GPT Image drew the detective from four sides, and Tripo turned the sheet into a rigged 3D model, 1.40 m tall. Claude left Tripo's stock animations unused and wrote all nine of his moves itself, from the run and the slide to a fall and a get-up, as a Blender script on the same rig.

The director and two crew members came the same way, each from a sheet of their own.

7The set

The street is a film set, so it was built like one: shop fronts on one side, bare timber and braces behind them, and an open side for the camera, the director and the crew. One Blender script of about 1,350 lines builds all 35 pieces from a single 64-colour palette, and it cost nothing to make. Each piece measured the same in Unreal as in Blender.

8A lab for the look

The lab put the real set, the hero, the director and two crew in one level, with the lighting values adjustable while it runs. The grey is built into the set's own materials, so the hero and the hazards can keep their colour, and the cut spreads out from the director's chair as a circle of light.

9The first full run

I played the lab and the test level, and had two big notes.

The plan had been a browser game. Dropping it let the game use Unreal's full desktop renderer, with Lumen and ray tracing. By the end of the first afternoon there was a complete run on the real set: a title screen, five takes on a 160 m street, a new note at every cut, and a result screen. It played, but it was dark and silent, and plain words stood in for the art.

That evening another agent relit the film: shadows from the lanterns, a light that follows the hero, light passing behind the set, a wet street, rain, steam and haze.

Part 4

Making it feel like a shoot

10Lights, camera, action

Now every take opens on the hero's face. The studio lights go off bank by bank, the film's lights come on, the camera swings round behind him and a clapperboard snaps shut on "Action!". Any key skips it. A failed take holds on the pratfall, then whips round to the director, furious, before the retake.

11Sound and music

ElevenLabs made all of it. The film has a fast noir chase score that winds down like a stopped reel at "Cut!" and spins back up at "Action!". The set gets a slow lounge piece between takes. Every hazard is heard before it hurts: a fuse hisses before a charge goes off, and a cable snaps before a light falls.

12The lens, the effects and a busy studio

Claude added explosions, sparks, splashes and rain, and gave the film an old lens: black bars, glowing highlights, scratches and a gently weaving frame. The studio got walls, a painted sky and eight crew walking about. The agent doing this work then suggested bringing the set camera down from 8.5 m up to just over the director's shoulder, and the crew and the lamps filled the frame.

13Hand-drawn lettering

GPT Image drew an alphabet on sheets, and a script cut the letters out, so every word and number on screen now uses the same hand-drawn letters. Keys and gamepad buttons got their own icons, and the title became a painted prop sign with paper lanterns.

Part 5

The director's notes

14Gunmen behind the barriers

Playing it, I noticed there was little reason to move left or right.

Gunmen now hide behind the barriers. Each one picks the lane you're in, marks it on the street, and fires a moment later, so you have to get out of it. They're extras with prop guns, so a hit just ruins the take. Take 1 is now only the route, and the gunmen arrive with the director's first note, "Cue the bad guys!". The rain starts as a drizzle and only pours when he asks for it.

15The director's megaphone

The notes worked, but a new take looked like the same take again.

Now the director bellows each note through a megaphone, with the note's icon in his speech bubble. The icon then flies into the row of notes on screen, marked NEW!, as the next take opens, and he gets his own music while he talks. Getting him to hold the megaphone at his mouth took another pass, because his arms are short and his head is big.

Part 6

After playing it

16Teaching the controls

From here, my notes came from playing it.

Take 1 now teaches the controls. The first time you need each one, the run freezes mid-stride, shows what to press, and carries on when you press it. Finishing all five takes brings up "Course 1 complete!" with posters for the courses still to come. Claude also gave out one to three stars, which I found meaningless, so they came out again.

17Falling the right way

Running into a barrier used to throw the hero forward onto the crates, where he sat on top of them. With a new fall he bounces off, lands on his back in front of the barrier, shakes his head and gets up. A test now checks where every bone ends up.

18A proper front door

The title now asks for whatever you're holding: a key, a button or a tap. A menu offers Play and Options, where the tutorial can be turned off. With it on, the director opens the run himself: "Listen up! 5 takes to get it right! Each take I add something new!" The corner of the screen just says "Take 1 of 5".

Part 7

Where it ended up

19The version on itch.io

The game on itch.io is v1.1, a packaged Mac build. Along the way it also gained arrow keys in the prompts, a card between takes, animations that blend into each other, and lanterns, laundry and flags swaying over the street. The gameplay video shows a whole run, with the game's own sound.
